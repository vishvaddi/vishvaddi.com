A photo or render of a specialist finish is never enough to price it
accurately. Get a **physical control sample** in your hand before committing
to a number. The sample tells you things no image can — actual texture, sheen
variation, tone under different lighting, weight, batch consistency, and
fixing implications.

This applies to anything that isn't off-the-shelf: blackened steel,
microcement systems, terrazzo, limestone, custom fabric, custom timber stain,
leather wall panels, polished plaster, hand-fired tiles — anything described
with an artisanal qualifier ("hand-finished," "hand-blackened," "patinated,"
"limewashed").

## Why this matters

- **Pricing variance.** Different application systems for "microcement" can be
  2–3× apart in cost. The sample tells you the system.
- **Application method.** Spray vs trowel vs hand-applied changes labour rates
  significantly. The sample reveals the technique.
- **Substrate compatibility.** Some specialist finishes need specific
  substrates (cement board, fibre cement, marine ply). The sample comes with
  the supplier's spec sheet.
- **Defect tolerance.** Luxury brands will reject installations that look
  perfect to you because they don't match the sample. Holding the sample on
  site is your defence.
- **Sub-trade selection.** Many specialist finishes are applied by a small
  handful of approved applicators. The sample lets you identify and brief the
  correct one.

## Estimator's pre-mortem question

When pricing a project with any non-standard finish:

1. Has the client provided a physical control sample? If no, ask for one in
   writing.
2. If a sample isn't available, request the supplier's product code, system
   spec sheet, and the name of an approved applicator.
3. **Don't quote firm** on a finish you've only seen in images. Use a PC SUM
   with clear assumptions stated.
4. If the project proceeds and the sample is still outstanding, escalate
   before the long-lead procurement window closes.

## What this protects against

- Variations after fabrication ("the colour isn't right")
- Re-do costs for installations rejected on quality grounds
- Programme delays from sourcing the correct system mid-project
- Underbidding by a competitor who priced a cheaper system

The discipline costs nothing. The absence of it costs entire margins.
